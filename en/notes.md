## First Grade, Volume 1

### Unit 1: Understanding Numbers Within $5$ and Addition/Subtraction

#### Understanding $1\sim5$

**Definition 1.1.1** (Cardinal Number): A number can represent the count of a class of objects; for example, $1$ represents $1$ house, and $5$ represents $5$ ducks. However far you count is how many there are.

#### Comparing Size

**Definition 1.1.2** (Same Amount, More, Fewer): Compare two classes of objects by one-to-one correspondence: if both sides match up completely, it is called "the same amount"; if some are left over, that class is "more" and the other class is "fewer".

**Definition 1.1.3** (Equals Sign, Greater-Than Sign, Less-Than Sign): Symbols expressing the size relation between two numbers: the equals sign "$=$" (read "equals"), the greater-than sign "$>$" (read "is greater than"), the less-than sign "$<$" (read "is less than"), with the opening facing the larger number.

#### Which Position

**Definition 1.1.4** (Cardinal Number and Ordinal Number): "How many" tells the quantity, which is a cardinal number; "which position" tells the position in a line, which is an ordinal number. For example, when $5$ people line up, "there are $5$ people" is cardinal, and "standing $2$nd" is ordinal.

#### Decomposition and Composition

**Definition 1.1.5** (Decomposition and Composition of Numbers): A number can be split into two numbers, and two numbers can also combine to form this number. For example, $5$ can be split into $1$ and $4$, $2$ and $3$, $3$ and $2$, $4$ and $1$; conversely, these pairs all combine to make $5$.

#### Addition and Subtraction of $1\sim5$

**Definition 1.1.6** (Meaning of Addition): Combining two parts together to find the total uses addition. The plus sign "$+$"; for example, $3+1=4$, read "$3$ plus $1$ equals $4$." When calculating, you may count on from the first number.

**Definition 1.1.7** (Meaning of Subtraction): Removing one part from the total to find what remains uses subtraction. The minus sign "$-$"; for example, $4-1=3$, read "$4$ minus $1$ equals $3$." When calculating, you may count backward from the total.

#### Understanding $0$ and Addition/Subtraction

**Definition 1.1.8** (Meaning and Operations of 0): $0$ means "nothing at all"; it also represents the starting point on the number line. Adding $0$ to or subtracting $0$ from a number does not change it: $a+0=a$, $a-0=a$; subtracting a number from itself gives $0$: $a-a=0$.

### Unit 2: Understanding $6\sim10$ and Addition/Subtraction

#### Understanding $6\sim9$

**Definition 1.2.1** (Understanding 6–9): When counting, after $5$ comes $6$, and continuing gives $7, 8, 9$; represent each number with sticks, a counter, or pictures; know how to read and write them, and arrange them in order from smallest to largest.

#### Comparing Size, Which Position

**Definition 1.2.2** (Comparing Size, Which Position): In numerical order, the later number is larger than the earlier, e.g. $6>5$, $7>6$, $8>7$, $9>8$; "which position" tells the order (ordinal number), which is different from "how many" (cardinal number).

#### Decomposition and Composition

**Definition 1.2.3** (Decomposition and Composition): A number can be split into two numbers, and two numbers can also combine to form this number; split them in order and write them in pairs, neither repeating nor omitting. For example, $6=1+5=2+4=3+3$.

#### Addition and Subtraction of $6$ and $7$

**Definition 1.2.4** (Four Sentences from One Picture): From one picture one can write two addition sentences and two subtraction sentences; for example, $5+1=6$, $1+5=6$, $6-1=5$, $6-5=1$, all expressing the relationship among the three parts in the picture.

#### Understanding $10$

**Definition 1.2.5** (Understanding 10): $9$ plus $1$ is $10$; $10$ ones make $1$ ten; $0\sim10$ are arranged in order, with the later number larger than the earlier.

#### Addition and Subtraction of $10$

**Definition 1.2.6** (Addition and Subtraction of 10): Based on the composition of $10$, calculate $10$ plus a number, a number plus $10$,$10$ minus a number; e.g. $1+9=10$, $9+1=10$, $10-1=9$, $10-9=1$.

#### Continuous Addition, Continuous Subtraction

**Definition 1.2.7** (Continuous Addition): Adding three numbers consecutively: from left to right, first add the first two, then add the third; e.g. $5+2+1=8$.

**Definition 1.2.8** (Continuous Subtraction): Subtracting two numbers consecutively from a number: from left to right, subtract one at a time; e.g. $8-2-2=4$.

#### Mixed Addition and Subtraction

**Definition 1.2.9** (Mixed Addition and Subtraction): An expression containing both addition and subtraction, calculated from left to right; e.g. $4+3-2=5$.

### Unit 3: Recognizing Solid Shapes

#### Recognizing Solid Shapes

**Definition 1.3.1** (Cuboid and Cube): A cuboid has flat faces and sits steadily on a desk; a cube has square faces of the same size, and also has flat faces.

**Definition 1.3.2** (Cylinder and Sphere): A cylinder has circular top and bottom faces; it can stand upright or roll; a sphere can roll in all directions.

### Unit 4: Understanding $11\sim20$

#### Re-Understanding $10$

**Definition 1.4.1** (10 Ones Make 1 Ten): $10$ sticks are bundled into $1$ bunch, and $10$ ones make $1$ ten; in $10$, the "$1$" represents $1$ ten, and the "$0$" represents that there are no ones in the ones place.

#### Understanding $11\sim20$

**Definition 1.4.2** (Ones Place and Tens Place): From the right, the first digit is the ones place, and the second is the tens place; how many tens there are, write that number in the tens place, and how many ones there are, write that number in the ones place.

**Definition 1.4.3** (Composition of Numbers 11–20): $1$ ten plus some ones makes a ten-something; $2$ tens is $20$; $11\sim20$ are arranged in order, with the later number larger than the earlier.

#### Simple Addition and Subtraction

**Definition 1.4.4** (Ten-Something Plus a Number and Corresponding Subtraction): $10$ plus a number gives a ten-something; ten-something minus a number gives $10$; ten-something minus $10$ gives the number; addend $+$ addend $=$ sum, minuend $-$ subtrahend $=$ difference.

### Unit 5: Carrying Addition Within $20$

#### $9$ Plus Something

**Definition 1.5.1** (Make-a-Ten Method): When calculating carrying addition, make one addend up to $10$, then add the remaining part. For example, for $9+4$, split $4$ into $1$ and $3$; $9+1=10$, $10+3=13$.

#### $8$, $7$, $6$ Plus Something

**Definition 1.5.2** (Swapping Addends): Swapping the two addends does not change the sum. For example, $8+5=13$, $5+8=13$; you may use the larger plus the smaller to calculate.

#### Solving Problems

**Definition 1.5.3** (Using Addition to Solve Problems): To find the total or "how many there were originally," combine two parts with addition; e.g. the $6$ taken out plus the $5$ remaining gives $6+5=11$.

### Unit 6: Review and Connection

#### Numbers and Operations

**Definition 1.6.1** (Understanding Numbers Within 20): $0\sim20$ are arranged in order, with the later number larger than the earlier; $10$ ones make $1$ ten; from the right, the first digit is the ones place, the second is the tens place; how many tens there are, write that number in the tens place, and how many ones there are, write that number in the ones place.

**Definition 1.6.2** (Addition and Subtraction Within 20): Combine two parts with addition, and remove a part from the total with subtraction; use the make-a-ten method for carrying addition, making one addend up to $10$ first.

#### Recognizing Shapes

**Definition 1.6.3** (Solid Shapes): Cuboids and cubes have flat faces; cylinders can stand upright or roll; spheres can roll in all directions.

## First Grade, Volume 2

### Unit 1: Recognizing Plane Shapes

#### Recognizing Plane Shapes

**Definition 2.1.1** (Plane Shapes): From solid shapes one can find plane shapes such as rectangles, squares, parallelograms, triangles, and circles; their faces are flat.

#### Piece It Together

**Definition 2.1.2** (Combining Shapes): Several identical plane shapes can form a new plane shape, and one plane shape can also be divided into several plane shapes. For example, $2$ identical rectangles can form a new rectangle, and $2$ identical triangles can form a parallelogram.

#### Using the Tangram

**Definition 2.1.3** (Tangram): The tangram is a traditional Chinese puzzle toy consisting of $7$ pieces, with which one can form a great variety of patterns.

### Unit 2: Regrouping Subtraction Within $20$

#### Ten-Something Minus $9$

**Definition 2.2.1** (Ten-Something Minus $9$): To calculate ten-something minus $9$, you may "think addition to do subtraction"; e.g. think $9+6=15$, so $15-9=6$. You may also use the "break-ten method": first calculate $10-9=1$, then add the ones digit.

#### Ten-Something Minus $8$, $7$, $6$

**Definition 2.2.2** (Regrouping Subtraction): When the ones digit is not enough to subtract, borrow $1$ from the tens place as $10$, combine it with the ones digit, then subtract.

#### Solving Problems

**Definition 2.2.3** (Solving Word Problems): After reading the problem, first identify the given conditions and the question, select the conditions relevant to the question to write the sentence, do not use irrelevant conditions in the calculation, and check afterward.

### Unit 3: Understanding Numbers Within $100$

#### Counting and Number Composition

**Definition 2.3.1** (Counting and Number Composition): $10$ ones make $1$ ten, and $10$ tens make one hundred. Some tens and some ones combine to make a ten-something; for example, $4$ tens and $6$ ones combine to make forty-six.

#### Reading and Writing Numbers

**Definition 2.3.2** (Place Value): From the right, the first digit is the ones place, the second is the tens place, and the third is the hundreds place. Reading and writing numbers both start from the highest place.

#### Number Order and Comparing Size

**Definition 2.3.3** (Comparing Two-Digit Numbers): To compare two two-digit numbers, first compare the tens digit; the number with the larger tens digit is larger. If the tens digits are the same, then compare the ones digit.

**Definition 2.3.4** (A Little More and Much More): When two numbers differ little, say "a little larger" or "a little smaller"; when they differ greatly, say "much larger" or "much smaller."

#### Simple Addition and Subtraction

**Definition 2.3.5** (Round-Tens Plus a One-Digit Number and Corresponding Subtraction): Some tens and some ones combine to make a ten-something; e.g. $30+2=32$, $32-2=30$, $32-30=2$.

### Unit 4: Mental Addition and Subtraction Within $100$

#### Mental Addition

**Definition 2.4.1** (Mental Addition): In mental addition, add the ones to the ones and the tens to the tens; when the ones sum to ten or more, carry $1$ to the tens place.

#### Mental Subtraction

**Definition 2.4.2** (Mental Subtraction): In mental subtraction, subtract the ones from the ones and the tens from the tens; when the ones digit is not enough to subtract, borrow $1$ from the tens place as $10$ and then subtract.

**Definition 2.4.3** (Solving Problems with Continuous Subtraction): "Putting several in one portion, asking how many portions can be filled and how many remain," you may circle out $8$ groups of $8$, or subtract consecutively.

### Unit 5: Written Addition and Subtraction Within $100$

#### Written Addition

**Definition 2.5.1** (Vertical Form): Written addition and subtraction use vertical form: align the same place values, and calculate from the ones place.

**Definition 2.5.2** (Written Addition): When the ones sum to ten or more, carry $1$ to the tens place.

#### Written Subtraction

**Definition 2.5.3** (Written Subtraction): When the ones digit is not enough to subtract, borrow $1$ from the tens place.

### Unit 6: Addition-Subtraction Relationships Between Quantities

#### Comparing More and Less

**Definition 2.6.1** (Solving Problems with Addition): Knowing two parts and asking for the total uses addition; knowing one number and "how much more it is," asking for the larger number, also uses addition.

**Definition 2.6.2** (Solving Problems with Subtraction): Knowing the total and one part and asking for the other part uses subtraction; asking how much more (or less) one number is than another, subtract the smaller from the larger.

**Definition 2.6.3** (Comparing More and Less): To find the number that is several more than a given number, use addition; to find the number that is several less than a given number, use subtraction; drawing pictures can make the quantitative relationships clearer.

### Unit 7: Happy Shopping Street

#### Recognizing Renminbi

**Definition 2.7.1** (Renminbi Units): Renminbi is the legal tender of our country, in both banknotes and coins. The units of Renminbi are yuan, jiao, and fen.

#### Exchange It

**Definition 2.7.2** (Conversion of Yuan, Jiao, and Fen): $1$ yuan $=10$ jiao, $1$ jiao $=10$ fen.

**Definition 2.7.3** (Renminbi Exchange and Change): $1$ $20$-yuan note can be exchanged for $2$ $10$-yuan notes or $4$ $5$-yuan notes; when shopping, pay correctly and learn to make change.

### Unit 8: Review and Connection

#### Numbers and Operations

**Definition 2.8.1** (Numbers Within 100): $2$ tens and $5$ ones combine to make $25$; $9$ tens and $9$ ones combine to make $99$, and adding $1$ more gives $100$, which is $1$ hundred.

**Definition 2.8.2** (Addition and Subtraction Within 100): When calculating, add and subtract the ones with the ones and the tens with the tens; when the ones sum to ten, carry $1$; when not enough, borrow $1$.

#### Application and Improvement

**Definition 2.8.3** (Quantitative Relationships): Addition and subtraction can solve problems of how many in total, how many remain, and comparing more and less.

## Second Grade, Volume 1

### Unit 1: Classification and Organization

#### Classification

**Definition 3.1.1** (Classification): Dividing things into several groups according to a certain standard; things in the same group have the same characteristics under this standard. For example, classify balloons by shape, or vegetables by type.

**Definition 3.1.2** (Representing Classification Results): You may represent the classification results by counting, drawing, filling in tables, etc., so that the quantity of each category is clear at a glance.

**Definition 3.1.3** (Different Standards, Different Results): Different classification standards may give different results, but the total number of classified things does not change. For example, classifying by adults and children vs. by gender gives different results, but the total number of people is the same.

**Definition 3.1.4** (Layer-by-Layer Classification): After dividing things into several classes by one standard, you may further divide within each class by another standard; continuing layer by layer makes the result more useful.

### Unit 2: Multiplication Tables of $1\sim6$

#### Introduction to Multiplication

**Definition 3.2.1** (Meaning of Multiplication): To find the sum of several identical addends, you may use multiplication. For example, $5$ times $3$ added together is written as $3\times5=15$, read "$3$ times $5$ equals $15$".

**Definition 3.2.2** (Names of Parts of a Multiplication Expression): The numbers on both sides of the multiplication sign are called multipliers, and the result is called the product. For example, in $3\times5=15$, $3$ and $5$ are multipliers, and $15$ is the product.

#### Multiplication Rhymes of $2\sim6$

**Definition 3.2.3** (Multiplication Rhymes): Rhyming phrases composed for quick multiplication, e.g. "two three six" "three five fifteen." One rhyme usually lets you calculate two multiplication expressions.

#### Multiply-Add, Multiply-Subtract

**Definition 3.2.4** (Multiply-Add, Multiply-Subtract): Expressions like $3\times3+2$,$3\times4-1$ are called multiply-add and multiply-subtract; in such expressions, calculate the multiplication first, then the addition or subtraction.

#### Solving Problems

**Definition 3.2.5** (Solving Problems with Multiplication): To find how much several groups of something is, use multiplication; problems involving repeated addition of the same addend can all be solved efficiently with multiplication.

### Unit 3: Division Tables of $1\sim6$

#### Introduction to Division

**Definition 3.3.1** (Equal Sharing): Giving each portion the same amount is called equal sharing. For example, dividing $6$ candies into $3$ portions, $2$ per portion, is equal sharing.

**Definition 3.3.2** (Meaning of Division): The process of equal sharing can be represented by division. For example, $12$ bamboo shoots, $4$ per plate, fill $3$ plates, written as $12\div4=3$, read "$12$ divided by $4$ equals $3$".

**Definition 3.3.3** (Names of Parts of a Division Expression): The number before the division sign is the dividend, the number after is the divisor, and the result is the quotient. For example, in $12\div4=3$, $12$ is the dividend, $4$ is the divisor, and $3$ is the quotient.

#### Using Multiplication Rhymes to Find the Quotient

**Definition 3.3.4** (Using Multiplication Rhymes to Find the Quotient): When calculating division, think "which number multiplied by the divisor gives the dividend"; the result of that multiplication rhyme is the quotient. For example, for $12\div3$, think "three (four) twelve," the quotient is $4$.

#### Multiplication-Division Relationship

**Definition 3.3.5** (Multiplication-Division Relationship): Multiplication and division are related and can be calculated with the same multiplication rhyme. For example, from "three four twelve" you calculate $3\times4=12$, $4\times3=12$, $12\div3=4$, $12\div4=3$.

### Unit 4: Campus Tour Guide

#### Recognizing East, South, West, North

**Definition 3.4.1** (East, South, West, North): Four basic directions. In the morning facing the sun, front is east, back is west, left is north, right is south; you may also use a compass to identify directions.

**Definition 3.4.2** (Relative Direction Relationships): East is opposite west, and south is opposite north. Once one direction is determined, the other three are determined.

**Definition 3.4.3** (Directions on a Map): Maps are drawn with north at the top, south at the bottom, west on the left, east on the right; on different direction signs, the arrangement of east, south, west, north follows clockwise order and is constant.

### Unit 5: Centimeters and Meters

#### Centimeters

**Definition 3.5.1** (Centimeters): "Centimeter" is a unified length unit, written "$\mathrm{cm}$". To measure shorter objects, use centimeters. The width of an index finger or a sticky note is about $1$ centimeter.

#### Meters

**Definition 3.5.2** (Meters): To measure longer objects, usually use "meters," written "$\mathrm{m}$". $1$ meter $=100$ centimeters.

#### Line Segments

**Definition 3.5.3** (Line Segments): A taut string can be regarded as a line segment. A line segment is straight and its length can be measured. The edges of a blackboard, a desk, or a book can all be regarded as line segments.

**Definition 3.5.4** (Measuring Method): When measuring an object, align the $0$ mark of the ruler with one end of the object, then see which mark the other end reaches; that is the length in centimeters.

### Unit 6: Rulers on the Body

#### Body Lengths and Body Rulers

**Definition 3.6.1** (Body Rulers): Span, step, arm span, foot, finger, hand, elbow, etc. are length units used in ancient times, also called "body rulers." Without a ruler or when a ruler is inconvenient, you may choose a suitable body ruler to measure length.

**Definition 3.6.2** (1 Span): Spread the fingers wide; the distance from the tip of the thumb to the tip of the middle finger is $1$ span.

**Definition 3.6.3** (1 Arm Span): Extend both arms horizontally; the distance between the tips of the two middle fingers is $1$ arm span; the length of $1$ arm span is about equal to one's height.

### Unit 7: Multiplication and Division Tables of $7\sim9$

#### Multiplication Rhymes of $7$

**Definition 3.7.1** (Multiplication Rhymes of 7): One seven is seven, two sevens fourteen, three sevens twenty-one, four sevens twenty-eight, five sevens thirty-five, six sevens forty-two, seven sevens forty-nine. The products of adjacent rhymes differ by $7$; one rhyme generally lets you calculate two multiplication expressions.

#### Multiplication Rhymes of $8$

**Definition 3.7.2** (Multiplication Rhymes of 8): One eight is eight, two eighths sixteen, three eighths twenty-four, four eighths thirty-two, five eighths forty, six eighths forty-eight, seven eighths fifty-six, eight eighths sixty-four. The products of adjacent rhymes differ by $8$.

#### Multiplication Rhymes of $9$

**Definition 3.7.3** (Multiplication Rhymes of 9): One nine is nine, two nines eighteen, three nines twenty-seven, four nines thirty-six, five nines forty-five, six nines fifty-four, seven nines sixty-three, eight nines seventy-two, nine nines eighty-one. Several nines is several tens minus that number; e.g. $9\times5$ is $50-5=45$; the sum of the tens digit and ones digit of the product is always $9$.

#### Finding the Quotient Using Multiplication Rhymes of $7\sim9$

**Definition 3.7.4** (Finding the Quotient Using Multiplication Rhymes of 7–9): When calculating division with divisor $7, 8, 9$, think "which number multiplied by the divisor gives the dividend"; the missing multiplier in that rhyme is the quotient. For example, for $56\div8$, think "seven eight fifty-six," the quotient is $7$.

#### Solving Problems

**Definition 3.7.5** (Solving Two-Step Problems with Multiplication and Division): First use multiplication to find the total, then use division to find the amount per portion or the number of portions; the result of the first step is the condition for the second step; both steps are indispensable.

#### Organization and Review

**Definition 3.7.6** (Multiplication Rhyme Table): The small multiplication rhyme table has $45$ rhymes in a triangular arrangement, from "one one is one" to "nine nine eighty-one"; except for rhymes where both multipliers are the same, each rhyme lets you calculate two multiplication expressions and two division expressions.

### Unit 8: Review and Connection

#### Numbers and Operations

**Definition 3.8.1** (Organization of Multiplication-Division Relationships): Addition with identical addends is more concisely expressed as multiplication; the process of equal sharing is more concisely expressed as division; multiplication and division are inverse operations, and one multiplication rhyme can calculate two multiplication expressions and two division expressions. For example, from "two three six" calculate $2\times3=6$, $3\times2=6$, $6\div2=3$, $6\div3=2$.

#### Quantitative Relationships

**Definition 3.8.2** (Quantitative Relationships): Given the amount per portion and the number of portions, find the total with multiplication: amount per portion $\times$ number of portions $=$ total; given the total and the amount per portion, find the number of portions with division; given the total and the number of portions, find the amount per portion also with division.

#### Shape Position and Measurement

**Definition 3.8.3** (Organization of Directions and Measurement): Learned the four directions east, south, west, north; maps are drawn with north up, south down, west left, east right; length units include centimeters and meters, $1$ meter $=100$ centimeters; line segments are straight and can be measured; also learned body rulers such as span, step, and arm span.

#### Classification and Organization

**Definition 3.8.4** (Organization of Classification and Organization): Classification must follow a certain standard; different standards may give different results, but the total number of classified things does not change; you may continue classifying the first result by another standard.

## Second Grade, Volume 2

### Unit 1: Where Is Time

#### Telling Time

**Definition 4.1.1** (Clock Face Structure): The clock face has three hands: hour, minute, and second, as well as grids and numbers. The clock face is divided by the numbers into $12$ large divisions, each large division into $5$ small divisions, for a total of $60$ small divisions on the clock face.

**Definition 4.1.2** (Hour, Minute, Second): The hour hand moving $1$ large division is $1$ hour; the minute hand moving $1$ small division is $1$ minute; the second hand moving $1$ small division is $1$ second. Hours, minutes, and seconds are units for recording time.

**Definition 4.1.3** (Rates Between Hours and Minutes, Minutes and Seconds): The hour hand going $1$ full circle is $12$ hours; the minute hand going $1$ full circle is $60$ minutes; the second hand going $1$ full circle is $60$ seconds. $1$ hour $=60$ minutes, $1$ minute $=60$ seconds. In daily life, $30$ minutes is called half an hour, and $30$ seconds is called half a minute.

**Definition 4.1.4** (Reading Hours and Minutes): To read the time on a clock, first see which hour the hour hand has passed, then see how many small divisions the minute hand has moved from $12$. When the hour hand points to $7$ and the minute hand to $12$, it is $7$ o'clock; e.g. $4$ o'clock $5$ minutes, $4$ o'clock $30$ minutes (half past $4$), $4$ o'clock $45$ minutes.

### Unit 2: Division with Remainder

#### Understanding Division with Remainder

**Definition 4.2.1** (Remainder): After equally sharing some objects, if there are leftovers, the leftover part is the remainder. For example, $9\div4=2$ (objects) $\cdots\cdots$ $1$ (stick), read "$9$ divided by $4$ equals $2$ remainder $1$"; the leftover $1$ is called the remainder.

#### Relationship Between Remainder and Divisor

**Definition 4.2.2** (Remainder Must Be Less Than Divisor): When equal sharing has leftovers, the remainder must be less than the divisor. For example, using sticks to form triangles (divisor is $3$), you might have $1$ or $2$ sticks left, but never $3$ or more.

#### Vertical Form of Division with Remainder

**Definition 4.2.3** (Relationships Among Parts of Division with Remainder): Dividend $\div$ divisor $=$ quotient $\cdots\cdots$ remainder, and dividend $=$ divisor $\times$ quotient $+$ remainder. For example, $13\div4=3\cdots\cdots1$, $13=4\times3+1$.

#### Estimating the Quotient

**Definition 4.2.4** (Estimating the Quotient): When finding the quotient, think of which number times the divisor gives a product close to but less than the dividend; that number is the quotient; after calculating, check whether the remainder is less than the divisor.

### Unit 3: Multiplication-Division Relationships Between Quantities

#### Understanding Multiples

**Definition 4.3.1** (Meaning of Multiples): Comparing two quantities, regard the smaller as $1$ portion; if the larger has several such portions, the larger is several times the smaller. For example, $2$ geese, and chickens have $3$ groups of $2$; the number of chickens is $3$ times the number of geese.

#### Finding How Many Times One Number Is Another

**Definition 4.3.2** (Finding How Many Times One Number Is Another): To find how many times number A is number B, find how many B's are in A; use division. For example, $12\div4=3$.

#### Finding Several Times a Number

**Definition 4.3.3** (Finding Several Times a Number): To find several times a number, find how many groups of that number; use multiplication. For example, $4$ times $8$ is $4$ groups of $8$, $8\times4=32$.

#### Given the Multiple, Find $1$ Portion

**Definition 4.3.4** (Given the Multiple, Find $1$ Portion): Knowing that one number is several times another and knowing that number, to find $1$ portion, use division. For example, $6$ portions is $12$ meters; to find $1$ portion, use $12\div6=2$ meters.

### Unit 4: Understanding Numbers Within 10000

#### Understanding Numbers Within $1000$

**Definition 4.4.1** (Decimal Relationship of Counting Units): Counting one by one, $10$ ones is ten; counting by tens, $10$ tens is one hundred; $10$ hundreds is one thousand; $10$ thousands is ten thousand. Ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, and ten-thousands are all counting units; adjacent ones differ by a factor of $10$.

#### Understanding Numbers Within $10000$

**Definition 4.4.2** (Place Value Order): From the right: ones place, tens place, hundreds place, thousands place, ten-thousands place…… whatever digit is in a place means that many of that counting unit. For example, the $5$ in the hundreds place means $5$ hundreds.

#### Abacus

#### Reading and Writing Numbers Within 10000

**Definition 4.4.3** (Reading Numbers Within 10000): Read from the highest place by place value: read thousands as so many thousands, hundreds as so many hundreds…… one $0$ or two $0$s in the middle are read as only one "zero"; any number of $0$s at the end are not read.

**Definition 4.4.4** (Writing Numbers Within 10000): Write from the highest place to the lowest. If a digit has no unit, write $0$ there.

#### Comparing Size of Numbers Within 10000

**Definition 4.4.5** (Comparing Numbers Within 10000): Different number of digits: the one with more digits is larger; same number of digits: compare from the highest digit; larger highest digit means larger; if same, compare the next digit, until size is determined.

#### Approximate Numbers

**Definition 4.4.6** (Approximate Numbers and Approximation Sign): In daily life, sometimes approximate numbers are used instead of exact numbers. Numbers close to a round thousand or hundred can be written as approximations, connected by the approximation sign $\approx$, read as "approximately equal to." For example, $6988\approx7000$.

### Unit 5: Addition and Subtraction Within 10000

#### Addition

**Definition 4.5.1** (Written Addition Within 10000): Align the same place values, add from the ones place; when a digit sums to ten or more, carry $1$ to the next place. Aligning the same place values ensures that the counts of the same counting unit are added.

#### Subtraction

**Definition 4.5.2** (Written Subtraction Within 10000): Align the same place values, subtract from the ones place; when a digit is not enough to subtract, borrow $1$ from the previous place, add $10$ to this place, then subtract.

#### Relationships Among Parts of Addition and Subtraction

**Definition 4.5.3** (Relationships Among Parts of Addition): The operation of merging two numbers into one is called addition. Sum $=$ addend $+$ addend; addend $=$ sum $-$ the other addend.

**Definition 4.5.4** (Relationships Among Parts of Subtraction): The operation of finding one addend given the sum of two addends and one of them is called subtraction; subtraction is the inverse operation of addition. Difference $=$ minuend $-$ subtrahend; subtrahend $=$ minuend $-$ difference; minuend $=$ subtrahend $+$ difference.

### Unit 6: Mathematical Picture Stories

#### Picture Story Sharing Session

**Definition 4.6.1** (Mathematical Picture Stories): Use several connected pictures to tell a story containing mathematical information or quantitative relationships; each picture has text explanation, and mathematical questions can be raised from it.

**Definition 4.6.2** (Division Situations in Picture Stories): Express the quantitative relationship in the picture story with an expression. For example, bought $11$ cans, eating $5$ per day, $11\div5=2$ (days) $\cdots\cdots$ $1$ (can), meaning enough for $2$ days with $1$ can left.

### Unit 7: Review and Connection

#### Organization and Review

**Definition 4.7.1** (Organization of Numbers and Operations): Building on ones, tens, and hundreds, also learned thousands and ten-thousands; adjacent counting units differ by a factor of $10$. The composition, reading/writing, order, size comparison, and approximation of numbers within 10000 follow the same methods as for numbers within 100.

**Definition 4.7.2** (Organization of Division and Multiplication-Division Relationships): If equal sharing has no remainder, use table division; if there is a remainder, use division with remainder, and the remainder must be less than the divisor; to find how many times one number is another, use division; to find several times a number, use multiplication; the key is to identify which quantity is taken as $1$ portion.

**Definition 4.7.3** (Organization of Addition-Subtraction Relationships): Addition and subtraction are inverse operations: sum $=$ addend $+$ addend; minuend $=$ subtrahend $+$ difference; these relationships can be used to check and find unknowns.

## Third Grade, Volume 1

### Unit 1: Observing Objects

#### Observing Objects from Different Directions

**Definition 5.1.1** (Observing Objects from Different Directions): Observing the same object from different directions (front, top, side), the shapes seen are often different. To judge which position a picture was seen from, grasp the features of the face seen from that direction.

**Definition 5.1.2** (Inferring Solid Shapes from Seen Faces): Looking at a solid shape from one direction, you see only one of its faces. If one face seen is a square, the solid shape could be a cube, or another solid shape with one square face.

#### Nets of a Cuboid

**Definition 5.1.3** (Nets of a Cuboid): Cut a cuboid box along its edges and lay it flat; the resulting plane figure is the net of the cuboid. A cuboid has $6$ faces, and opposite faces have the same shape and size; cutting different edges gives different net shapes, and each face in the net has at least one edge connected to another face.

### Unit 2: Mixed Operations

#### Same-Level Operation Order

**Definition 5.2.1** (Combined Expressions and Same-Level Order): Merging two step-by-step expressions into one is a combined expression. In an expression without parentheses, if there are only addition and subtraction, or only multiplication and division, calculate from left to right in order.

#### Multiply-Divide Before Add-Subtract

**Definition 5.2.2** (Multiply-Divide Before Add-Subtract): In an expression without parentheses, if there are both multiplication/division and addition/subtraction, calculate multiplication/division first, then addition/subtraction.

#### Parentheses

**Definition 5.2.3** (Parentheses): If an expression has parentheses, calculate inside the parentheses first. Parentheses can change the order of operations.

#### Solving Multi-Step Word Problems

**Definition 5.2.4** (Solving Multi-Step Word Problems): To solve a multi-step problem, usually decompose it into several related smaller problems, solving one at a time; you may draw line segment diagrams to analyze quantitative relationships and write a combined expression.

### Unit 3: Millimeters, Decimeters, and Kilometers

#### Millimeters and Decimeters

**Definition 5.3.1** (Relationship Between Millimeters and Centimeters): The millimeter (mm) is a smaller length unit than the centimeter; use it for short objects or when precise measurement is needed. On a ruler, the length of each small division between $1$ centimeter marks is $1$ millimeter; $1$ centimeter $=10$ millimeters.

**Definition 5.3.2** (Relationship Between Decimeters, Centimeters, and Meters): The decimeter (dm) is a length unit larger than centimeters and smaller than meters. On a ruler, $10$ centimeters is $1$ decimeter; $1$ decimeter $=10$ centimeters, $1$ meter $=10$ decimeters.

#### Kilometers

**Definition 5.3.3** (Kilometers): To measure longer distances, usually use kilometers (km); $1$ kilometer $=1000$ meters. Kilometers are also called "kilometers" in daily life.

#### Organization of Length Units

**Definition 5.3.4** (Rates and Estimation of Length Units): Length units from largest to smallest: kilometer, meter, decimeter, centimeter, millimeter. Between adjacent units, except kilometer-to-meter which is $1000$, all are $10$. To estimate a distance, first choose a length as a standard.

### Unit 4: The Story of Cao Cao Weighing the Elephant

#### The Principle of Weighing the Elephant

**Definition 5.4.1** (Equality of Equals and Total Equals Sum of Parts): The principle of Cao Cao weighing the elephant: lead the elephant onto the boat and mark the waterline on the side, lead the elephant off and load stones until the boat sinks to the mark; at that point the elephant's mass equals the total mass of the stones. The stones are weighed in several batches, and the total mass of stones equals the sum of the masses weighed each time. Equals of equals are equal; the total equals the sum of its parts.

#### Mass Units

**Definition 5.4.2** (Grams and Kilograms): The gram (g) and kilogram (kg) are common units for measuring object mass. Use grams for lighter objects and kilograms for heavier objects. $1$ kilogram $=1000$ grams. Kilograms are also called "kilograms" in daily life.

**Definition 5.4.3** (Ton): To measure heavier or bulk goods, usually use tons (t). $1$ ton $=1000$ kilograms.

**Definition 5.4.4** (Using Known Mass as a Standard): Without a scale, you may use objects of known mass (e.g. bags of salt at $200$ g each) as a standard, and by hefting or equal replacement, weigh other objects. In daily life there are also units like kilogram, jin, and liang: $1$ kilogram $=1$ kg, $1$ jin $=500$ g, $1$ kilogram $=2$ jin, $1$ jin $=10$ liang.

### Unit 5: Multi-Digit Times One-Digit

#### Mental Multiplication

**Definition 5.5.1** (Mental Multiplication): Round-ten or round-hundred numbers times a one-digit number: first multiply the number before the $0$ by the one-digit, then append the corresponding number of $0$s to the product. Two-digit times one-digit: split the two-digit into a round-ten and a one-digit, multiply separately, then add.

#### Written Multiplication

**Definition 5.5.2** (Written Multiplication): Vertical form for multi-digit times one-digit: starting from the ones place, multiply each digit of the multi-digit by the one-digit in turn; when a product reaches several tens, carry that number to the next place.

#### Multiplication by $0$

**Definition 5.5.3** (Multiplication by 0): $0$ times any number is $0$.

**Definition 5.5.4** (Multiplication with 0 in the Middle or End): If a factor has $0$ in the middle, that digit must also be multiplied; if a factor ends in $0$, you may first multiply the number before the $0$, then append the same number of $0$s to the product.

#### Solving Problems with Estimation

**Definition 5.5.5** (Solving Problems with Estimation): Sometimes you only need to judge "is it enough," not an exact calculation; you may overestimate or underestimate: if you overestimate the number of people and the money is still enough, then it is definitely enough in reality.

### Unit 6: Number Coding

#### Recognizing Number Codes

**Definition 5.6.1** (Number Coding): There are many number codes in life; they are "identity numbers" people assign to things according to practical needs and certain standards, with each part representing specific information. A citizen identity number has $18$ digits: address code, birth date code, sequence code, and check code; the $17$th digit being odd means male, even means female; the check code is calculated from the first $17$ digits and can be $0\sim10$, written as Roman numeral $X$ when it is $10$.

#### Designing a Coding Scheme

**Definition 5.6.2** (Designing a Coding Scheme): To design a coding scheme, first determine what information to include (e.g. grade, class, serial number, gender), then assign fixed digit lengths and order to each part; when the coding range expands (whole class, whole school, whole city), the scheme must add digits to avoid duplicates.

### Unit 7: Lines and Angles

#### Line Segments, Rays, Lines

**Definition 5.7.1** (Line Segments, Rays, Lines): A taut string can be regarded as a line segment; a line segment has two endpoints and can be measured. Extending a line segment infinitely in one direction gives a ray, which has only one endpoint. Extending a line segment infinitely in both directions gives a line, which has no endpoints and is infinitely long. Among all connecting lines between two points, the line segment is shortest; its length is called the distance between the two points.

#### Angles

**Definition 5.7.2** (Angles): The figure formed by two rays drawn from one point is an angle; this point is the vertex, and the two rays are the sides. An angle is denoted by "$\angle$". The wider the two sides open, the larger the angle.

**Definition 5.7.3** (Right, Acute, Obtuse Angles): The angle on a triangular ruler is a right angle. An angle smaller than a right angle is an acute angle; an angle larger than a right angle is an obtuse angle. To check whether an angle is a right angle, compare it with the right angle on a triangular ruler.

### Unit 8: Introduction to Fractions

#### Understanding Fractions

**Definition 5.8.1** (Introduction to Fractions): Dividing an object equally into several parts, one part is one-over-something, and several parts are several-over-something. The horizontal line in a fraction is the fraction bar; the number below is the denominator (how many equal parts), and the number above is the numerator (how many parts are taken).

**Definition 5.8.2** (Comparing Fraction Size): Fractions with the same denominator: the one with the larger numerator is larger; fractions with the same numerator: the one with the larger denominator is smaller (the more equal parts, the smaller each part).

#### Simple Fraction Calculation

**Definition 5.8.3** (Addition and Subtraction of Fractions with the Same Denominator): Add or subtract fractions with the same denominator by keeping the denominator and adding or subtracting the numerators. $1$ can be written as a fraction with the same numerator and denominator, e.g. $1=\frac{4}{4}$.

#### Finding a Fraction of a Number

**Definition 5.8.4** (Finding a Fraction of a Number): To find one-over-something of a number, divide the number equally into that many parts and find one part; to find several-over-something, multiply one part by the numerator.

### Unit 9: Review and Connection

#### Organization of Numbers and Operations

**Definition 5.9.1** (Organization of Numbers and Operations): Multi-digit times one-digit can be converted to table multiplication: mentally split the number into thousands, hundreds, tens, and ones, then multiply and add; written multiplication multiplies from the ones place, carrying several tens. Mixed operations: same-level from left to right, multiply-divide before add-subtract, parentheses first. Fractions represent part-whole relationships; add/subtract fractions with the same denominator by keeping the denominator and adding/subtracting numerators.

#### Organization of Quantitative Relationships and Problem Solving

**Definition 5.9.2** (Organization of Quantitative Relationships and Problem Solving): To solve multi-step problems, first find the quantitative relationship; you may draw line segment diagrams, write step-by-step expressions or a combined expression; choose estimation or exact calculation as needed. Overestimate the number of people and the money is enough, so in reality it is definitely enough.

#### Organization of Common Quantities and Shapes

**Definition 5.9.3** (Organization of Common Quantities and Shapes): Length units: millimeter, centimeter, decimeter, meter, kilometer (adjacent rates $10$, $10$, $10$, $1000$); mass units: gram, kilogram, ton (all rate $1000$). Lines: segments, rays, lines; angles: acute, right, obtuse (acute $<$ right $<$ obtuse). Observing objects from different directions often gives different shapes.

#### Matching Problems

**Definition 5.9.4** (Matching Problems): If completing a task requires two steps with several choices at each step, use multiplication for the total; e.g. $2$ tops and $3$ bottoms give $2\times3=6$ outfits. If completing a task splits into two categories, use addition for the total; e.g. $2$ flights and $3$ high-speed trains give $2+3=5$ choices.

## Third Grade, Volume 2

### Unit 1: Motion Phenomena in Life

#### Axisymmetric Figures

**Definition 6.1.1** (Axisymmetric Figures and Axes of Symmetry): A figure folded along a straight line such that both sides match completely is an axisymmetric figure; the fold line is the axis of symmetry. By folding paper, discover that a rectangle has $2$ axes of symmetry and a square has $4$.

#### Translation

**Definition 6.1.2** (Translation): An object moving in a certain direction by a certain distance without changing shape, size, or direction is translation. For example, an elevator moving vertically up and down, or a child sliding diagonally down a slide.

#### Rotation

**Definition 6.1.3** (Rotation): Motion like windmill blades or a helicopter propeller, rotating around a point (center), is rotation. In the same direction as the hour hand on a clock is clockwise rotation; in the opposite direction is counterclockwise rotation.

### Unit 2: Division with a One-Digit Divisor

#### Mental Division

**Definition 6.2.1** (Mental Division): Round-ten or round-hundred numbers divided by a one-digit number: regard the dividend as several tens or hundreds and divide; two-digit divided by one-digit: split into round-ten and ones, divide separately, then add. For example, $60\div3=20$, $69\div3=23$, $600\div3=200$.

#### Division Estimation

**Definition 6.2.2** (Division Estimation): Regard the dividend as a nearby round-ten or round-hundred number and divide, estimating only the approximate result without calculating the exact value. For example, $283\div3$ is between $90$ and $100$.

#### Written Division

**Definition 6.2.3** (Written Division Rules): Divide from the highest place of the dividend: first try dividing the highest digit by the divisor; if it is smaller than the divisor, try the first two digits; write the quotient above whichever digit you've divided to; if the quotient would be $1$ but isn't enough, use $0$ as a placeholder; after finding each quotient digit, the remainder must be less than the divisor.

**Definition 6.2.4** (Checking Division): With no remainder, quotient $\times$ divisor $=$ dividend; with a remainder, quotient $\times$ divisor $+$ remainder $=$ dividend.

**Definition 6.2.5** (Division by 0): $0$ divided by any number that is not $0$ is $0$; $0$ cannot be a divisor.

#### Solving Problems

**Definition 6.2.6** (Solving Two-Step Problems with Multiplication and Division): Analyze the quantitative relationship; you may start from the conditions to think about what you can find, or start from the question to think about what to find first; write step-by-step or combined expressions. Normalization problems find the unit amount first; totalization problems find the total first.

### Unit 3: Rectangles and Squares

#### Polygons

**Definition 6.3.1** (Polygons): A closed figure bounded by line segments is a polygon; it has as many angles as sides. A triangle has $3$ sides, a quadrilateral has $4$, a pentagon has $5$, a hexagon has $6$. A polygon with all sides equal is a regular polygon.

#### Features of Rectangles and Squares

**Definition 6.3.2** (Features of Rectangles and Squares): A rectangle has opposite sides equal and $4$ right angles; a square has all $4$ sides equal and $4$ right angles. Folding a rectangle to make the largest square, the side length of the square equals the width of the rectangle.

#### Perimeter

**Definition 6.3.3** (Perimeter): The length around a closed figure is its perimeter. For a figure bounded by line segments, add up all the side lengths.

**Definition 6.3.4** (Perimeter of Rectangles and Squares): Rectangle perimeter $=$ (length $+$ width) $\times2$; square perimeter $=$ side length $\times4$.

### Unit 4: Area of Shapes

#### Area and Area Units

**Definition 6.4.1** (Area): The size of an object's surface or a closed figure is its area. Compare areas using a unified area unit, counting how many of those units fit.

**Definition 6.4.2** (Area Units): Common area units are square centimeters ($\mathrm{cm}^2$), square decimeters ($\mathrm{dm}^2$), and square meters ($\mathrm{m}^2$). A square with side $1$ cm has area $1$ square centimeter; a square with side $1$ dm has area $1$ square decimeter; a square with side $1$ m has area $1$ square meter.

#### Area of Rectangles and Squares

**Definition 6.4.3** (Area of Rectangles and Squares): Rectangle area $=$ length $\times$ width; square area $=$ side length $\times$ side length. To find area, count how many area units it contains.

#### Rates Between Area Units

**Definition 6.4.4** (Rates Between Area Units): The rate between adjacent common area units is $100$: $1$ square decimeter $=100$ square centimeters, $1$ square meter $=100$ square decimeters.

### Unit 5: Data Collection and Organization

#### Data Collection and Recording

**Definition 6.5.1** (Data Collection and Recording): Collect data by raising hands, filling out questionnaires, etc.; record by drawing tally characters: each tally character has $5$ strokes and represents $5$ items. Then fill the results into a table; each person selects only one item, without repetition or omission.

#### Two-Way Statistical Tables

**Definition 6.5.2** (Two-Way Statistical Tables): Combining two (or more) related one-way tables into one table can display multiple groups of data simultaneously, facilitating comparison and analysis.

#### Grouped Data Organization

**Definition 6.5.3** (Grouped Data Organization): When there is much data, divide it into groups by a certain standard; this is also classification. Using statistics to solve problems: first collect and organize data, then analyze, and finally draw a conclusion.

### Unit 6: The Secrets of Years, Months, Days

#### Years, Months, Days

**Definition 6.6.1** (Years, Months, Days): One year has $12$ months. Months with $31$ days are long months ($1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12$), months with $30$ days are short months ($4, 6, 9, 11$), and $2$ month (February) is neither. The time for the Earth to rotate once is $1$ day.

#### Common Years and Leap Years

**Definition 6.6.2** (Common Years and Leap Years): A year with $28$ days in $2$ month (February) is a common year; one with $29$ days is a leap year. A Gregorian year divisible by $4$ is usually a leap year; when the year is divisible by $100$, it must also be divisible by $400$ to be a leap year (e.g. $1900$ is not a leap year, $2000$ is).

#### $24$-Hour Timekeeping

**Definition 6.6.3** (24-Hour Timekeeping): $1$ day is $24$ hours; over one day the hour hand turns $2$ circles. Using $0$ to $24$ hours is the $24$-hour timekeeping method; for afternoon and evening times, add $12$ to the 12-hour clock time.

### Unit 7: Introduction to Decimals

#### Understanding Decimals

**Definition 6.7.1** (Understanding Decimals): Numbers like $3.45$,$0.85$,$2.60$,$36.6$ are decimals; "$.$" is the decimal point. $1$ decimeter $=\frac{1}{10}$ meter $=0.1$ meter, $1$ centimeter $=\frac{1}{100}$ meter $=0.01$ meter; $1$ jiao $=0.1$ yuan, $1$ fen $=0.01$ yuan.

#### Comparing Decimal Size

**Definition 6.7.2** (Comparing Decimal Size): First compare the integer part; the one with the larger integer part is larger; if the integer parts are the same, compare the decimal part digit by digit from the highest place.

#### Simple Decimal Addition and Subtraction

**Definition 6.7.3** (Simple Decimal Addition and Subtraction): In vertical form, align the decimal points (i.e. align the same units), then calculate as with integer addition/subtraction; the decimal point in the result must also align.

## Fourth Grade, Volume 1

### Unit 1: Understanding Numbers Beyond 10000

#### Understanding Numbers Within 100 Million

**Definition 7.1.1** (Counting Units and Digits): Ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten-thousands, hundred-thousands, millions, ten-millions, hundred-millions, etc. are all counting units. The rate between adjacent counting units is ten; this is the decimal system. Counting units arranged in order occupy positions called digits. From the right, every four digits form a level: ones level (ones, tens, hundreds, thousands), ten-thousands level, hundred-millions level.

**Definition 7.1.2** (Natural Numbers): $1, 2, 3, \cdots$ representing object counts are all natural numbers. Nothing is represented by $0$, and $0$ is also a natural number. The smallest natural number is $0$; there is no largest natural number; the count of natural numbers is infinite.

#### Reading and Writing Numbers Within 100 Million

**Definition 7.1.3** (Reading Large Numbers): First group by level, then read from the highest. Read the hundred-millions level, then the ten-thousands level, then the ones level; read the hundred-millions and ten-thousands levels like the ones level, then add "hundred million" or "ten thousand." Any number of $0$s at the end of a level are not read; one $0$ or several consecutive $0$s elsewhere are read as one "zero."

**Definition 7.1.4** (Writing Large Numbers): Write from the highest level to the lowest. If a digit has no unit, write $0$ there.

#### Comparing Number Size

**Definition 7.1.5** (Comparing Number Size): Different digit counts: more digits means larger. Same digit count: compare from the highest digit; larger highest digit means larger; if same, compare the next digit.

#### Number Rewriting and Approximation

**Definition 7.1.6** (Number Rewriting): To rewrite a round-ten-thousand number in "ten-thousand" units, remove the trailing $4$ $0$s and add "ten thousand"; to rewrite a round-hundred-million number in "hundred million" units, remove the trailing $8$ $0$s and add "hundred million." The value does not change; connect with "$=$".

**Definition 7.1.7** (Rounding to Approximate Numbers): When dropping the tail, look at the highest digit of the tail part: less than $5$ drops the tail (round down); $5$ or more carries $1$ to the previous place then drops the tail (round up). Connect the approximate and original numbers with "$\approx$".

### Unit 2: How Big Is $1$ Hundred Million

#### Comprehensive and Practical

**Definition 7.2.1** (Sampling and Estimation): When facing a huge number like $1$ hundred million that cannot be directly measured, first measure a small quantity (e.g. the thickness of $100$ sheets of paper, the mass of $100$ grains of rice), then use $1$ hundred million $=100000000=10000\times10000$ to estimate the corresponding quantity for $1$ hundred million.

### Unit 3: Angle Measurement

#### Re-Understanding Angles

**Definition 7.3.1** (Re-Understanding Angles): An angle can be seen as the figure formed by a ray rotating around its endpoint from one position to another. Rotating half a turn forms a straight angle; a full turn forms a full angle; a quarter turn forms a right angle.

**Definition 7.3.2** (Classification of Angles): Acute angle: $0^{\circ}<$ acute angle $<90^{\circ}$; right angle $=90^{\circ}$; obtuse angle: $90^{\circ}<$ obtuse angle $<180^{\circ}$; straight angle $=180^{\circ}$; full angle $=360^{\circ}$. $1$ full angle $=2$ straight angles $=4$ right angles.

#### Angle Measurement

**Definition 7.3.3** (Angle Measurement Unit): Divide a circle equally into $360$ parts; the angle subtended by $1$ part is the unit for measuring angles, and its size is $1$ degree, written $1^{\circ}$. A protractor is made by dividing a semicircle into $180$ equal parts.

**Definition 7.3.4** (Measuring Angles): Align the protractor's center with the angle's vertex, align the $0^{\circ}$ mark with one side, and the mark on the protractor along the other side is the angle's measure.

#### Drawing Angles

**Definition 7.3.5** (Drawing Angles): First draw a ray, align the protractor center with the ray's endpoint and the $0^{\circ}$ mark with the ray; mark a point at the target angle; using the ray's endpoint as endpoint, draw another ray through the marked point.

### Unit 4: Multi-Digit Times Two-Digit

#### Mental Multiplication

**Definition 7.4.1** (Mental Multiplication): Two-digit times one-digit can be split, e.g.

$$
16\times3=10\times3+6\times3=30+18=48.
$$

A hundred-something-ten number times a one-digit: first do two-digit times one-digit mentally, then append one $0$ to the product. Multiplying a number by $10$ appends one $0$.

#### Written Multiplication

**Definition 7.4.2** (Written Multiplication): Three-digit times two-digit: first multiply the three-digit by the ones digit of the two-digit, aligning the result's end with the ones place; then multiply by the tens digit, aligning the result's end with the tens place; then add the two products.

#### Product Change Patterns

**Definition 7.4.3** (Product Change Patterns): One multiplier stays the same; if the other multiplier is multiplied by (or divided by) some number (except $0$), the product is also multiplied by (or divided by) that number.

#### Multiplication Estimation

**Definition 7.4.4** (Multiplication Estimation): Based on the problem, overestimate or underestimate the multipliers to quickly judge the result range, then decide on exact calculation.

### Unit 5: Addition and Multiplication Models

#### Addition Model

**Definition 7.5.1** (Addition Model): Combining several parts to find the total uses addition: total $=$ part $+$ part. Given the total and one part, find the other part with subtraction: part $=$ total $-$ part.

#### Unit Price, Quantity, Total Price

**Definition 7.5.2** (Unit Price, Quantity, Total Price): The price per item is the unit price; how many you buy is the quantity; the total cost is the total price. Total price $=$ unit price $\times$ quantity; unit price $=$ total price $\div$ quantity; quantity $=$ total price $\div$ unit price.

#### Speed, Time, Distance

**Definition 7.5.3** (Speed, Time, Distance): Distance traveled per hour (or minute) is speed; total distance traveled is distance; hours (or minutes) traveled is time. Distance $=$ speed $\times$ time; speed $=$ distance $\div$ time; time $=$ distance $\div$ speed.

### Unit 6: Parallelograms and Trapezoids

#### Parallel and Perpendicular

**Definition 7.6.1** (Parallel and Perpendicular): Two lines in the same plane that never intersect are parallel lines; written $a\parallel b$. If two lines intersect at a right angle, they are perpendicular; one is the perpendicular of the other, and the intersection is the foot, written $a\perp b$.

**Definition 7.6.2** (Distance from a Point to a Line): Among all segments from a point outside a line to the line, the perpendicular segment is shortest; its length is the distance from the point to the line. The perpendicular segments between two parallel lines all have the same length.

#### Parallelograms

**Definition 7.6.3** (Parallelograms): A quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel opposite sides is a parallelogram. A parallelogram has equal opposite sides and equal opposite angles.

#### Trapezoids

**Definition 7.6.4** (Trapezoids): A quadrilateral with only one pair of parallel sides is a trapezoid. A trapezoid with equal legs is an isosceles trapezoid; one with right angles is a right trapezoid.

### Unit 7: Bar Graphs

#### One-Way Bar Graphs

**Definition 7.7.1** (Bar Graphs): A graph using bar lengths to represent quantities is a bar graph. It clearly shows quantities and facilitates comparison. When data is large, $1$ grid can represent multiple units (e.g. $2$, $5$, $10$), and half a grid represents half a unit.

#### Two-Way Bar Graphs

**Definition 7.7.2** (Two-Way Bar Graphs): A bar graph using two different bar styles (with a legend) to show two categories of data simultaneously is a two-way bar graph, facilitating comparison of two groups.

### Unit 8: Finding Treasure

#### Comprehensive and Practical

**Definition 7.8.1** (Eight Directions): Besides east, south, west, north, there are four more: northeast, northwest, southeast, southwest. Northeast is between east and north; southeast between east and south; northwest between west and north; southwest between west and south. Northeast is opposite southwest; northwest is opposite southeast.

**Definition 7.8.2** (Position and Direction): Describing an object's position requires direction and a reference point. When drawing a map, mark direction (north up, south down, west left, east right) and landmark positions. Use a compass to identify directions.

#### Organization and Review

**Definition 7.9.1** (Organization of Numbers and Operations): Understanding numbers beyond 100 million includes counting units and digits, reading/writing, comparing size, rewriting and approximation; multi-digit times two-digit includes mental, written, and product change patterns. ### Unit 1: Four Operations

#### Meaning and Relationships of Addition and Subtraction

**Definition 8.1.1** (Meaning and Relationships of Addition): The operation of merging two numbers into one is addition. The two numbers added are addends; the result is the sum. Sum $=$ addend $+$ addend; addend $=$ sum $-$ the other addend.

**Definition 8.1.2** (Meaning and Relationships of Subtraction): The operation of finding one addend given the sum and the other addend is subtraction. Subtraction is the inverse of addition. Difference $=$ minuend $-$ subtrahend; minuend $=$ subtrahend $+$ difference; subtrahend $=$ minuend $-$ difference.

#### Meaning and Relationships of Multiplication and Division

**Definition 8.1.3** (Meaning and Relationships of Multiplication): The efficient operation of finding the sum of several identical addends is multiplication. The two numbers multiplied are factors; the result is the product. Product $=$ factor $\times$ factor; factor $=$ product $\div$ the other factor.

**Definition 8.1.4** (Meaning and Relationships of Division): The operation of finding one factor given the product and the other factor is division. Division is the inverse of multiplication. Quotient $=$ dividend $\div$ divisor; divisor $=$ dividend $\div$ quotient; dividend $=$ quotient $\times$ divisor.

**Definition 8.1.5** (Operations with 0): A number plus $0$ is itself; when minuend equals subtrahend, the difference is $0$; a number times $0$ is $0$; $0$ divided by a number that is not $0$ is $0$. $0$ cannot be a divisor.

**Definition 8.1.6** (Relationships in Division with Remainder): In division with remainder, dividend $=$ quotient $\times$ divisor $+$ remainder, and the remainder must be less than the divisor.

#### Mixed Operations and Problem Solving

**Definition 8.1.7** (Order of Mixed Operations): Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division are collectively called the four operations. If an expression has both parentheses and brackets, calculate inside the parentheses first, then inside the brackets, then outside.

**Definition 8.1.8** (Strategies for Boat/Rental Problems): To solve how to rent boats (or cars) most cheaply, first calculate the per-seat price of each, prioritize renting the cheapest, then adjust to minimize empty seats, and compare several options.

### Unit 3: Operation Laws

#### Addition Operation Laws

**Definition 8.3.1** (Commutative Law of Addition): Adding two numbers, swapping the addends does not change the sum. In letters: $a+b=b+a$.

**Definition 8.3.2** (Associative Law of Addition): Adding three numbers, whether you add the first two first or the last two first, the sum is the same. In letters: $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$.

**Definition 8.3.3** (Subtraction Properties): A number subtracted consecutively by two numbers equals subtracting their sum: $a-b-c=a-(b+c)$; you may also subtract the second subtrahend first: $a-b-c=a-c-b$.

#### Multiplication Operation Laws

**Definition 8.3.4** (Commutative Law of Multiplication): Multiplying two numbers, swapping the factors does not change the product. In letters: $a\times b=b\times a$.

**Definition 8.3.5** (Associative Law of Multiplication): Multiplying three numbers, whether you multiply the first two first or the last two first, the product is the same. In letters: $(a\times b)\times c=a\times(b\times c)$.

**Definition 8.3.6** (Distributive Law): The sum of two numbers times a number can be multiplied separately then added. In letters: $(a+b)\times c=a\times c+b\times c$, and conversely $a\times c+b\times c=(a+b)\times c$.

**Definition 8.3.7** (Division Properties): A number divided consecutively by two numbers equals dividing by their product: $a\div b\div c=a\div(b\times c)$.

**Definition 8.3.8** (Efficient Calculation): Using addition and multiplication laws and subtraction and division properties, combine numbers that make round tens or hundreds first to make calculation easier.

**Definition 8.4.6** (Decimal Point Movement): Moving the decimal point right by one, two, three places multiplies the original number by $10$,$100$,$1000$…… enlarging to $10$,$100$,$1000$ times; moving left by one or two places divides by $10$,$100$…… shrinking to $\frac{1}{10}$,$\frac{1}{100}$.

### Unit 10: Math Corner — Finding the Defective Item

#### Finding the Defective Item

**Definition 10.10.1** (Optimal Strategy for Finding the Defective Item): Using a balance to find the defective item (only $1$ defective, known lighter or heavier), divide items into $3$ groups; divide evenly if possible, otherwise make the largest and smallest group differ by $1$. One weighing narrows the suspect range to $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the original.

**Definition 10.10.2** (Weighing Count Pattern): Guaranteed minimum weighings vs. item count: $2\sim3$ items need $1$ weighing, $4\sim9$ need $2$, $10\sim27$ need $3$, $28\sim81$ need $4$, $82\sim243$ need $5$.

## Sixth Grade, Volume 1

### Unit 1: Determining Position

#### Position

**Definition 11.1.1** (Ordered Pairs): Two numbers represent a point's position on a plane, written as (column, row); the order cannot be swapped. E.g. $(3, 4)$ means column $3$, row $4$.

**Definition 11.1.2** (Position by Direction and Distance): On a plane, first choose an observation point, then by the target's direction (e.g. $30^{\circ}$ east of north) and distance, the position is determined.

### Unit 2: Fraction Multiplication

#### Fraction Times Integer

**Definition 11.2.1** (Fraction Times Integer): Fraction times integer: numerator times integer is the new numerator, denominator unchanged. Reduce first if possible. Meaning is the same as integer multiplication: efficiently finding the sum of identical addends.

#### Number Times Fraction

**Definition 11.2.2** (Number Times Fraction): A number times a fraction means finding what fraction of that number; use multiplication. Fraction times fraction: numerator product over denominator product.

#### Fraction Mixed Operations and Laws

**Definition 11.2.3** (Fraction Mixed Operations and Laws): Order is the same as integers; integer multiplication laws apply to fractions too and can simplify calculation.

#### Finding a Fraction of a Number Repeatedly

**Definition 11.2.4** (Finding a Fraction of a Number Repeatedly): To find a fraction of a number repeatedly, use continuous multiplication. At each step, identify which quantity is unit "$1$"; note that unit "$1$" may change at each step.

### Unit 3: Fraction Division

#### Reciprocals

**Definition 11.3.1** (Reciprocals): Two numbers whose product is $1$ are reciprocals. To find the reciprocal (except $0$), swap numerator and denominator. The reciprocal of $1$ is $1$; $0$ has no reciprocal.

#### Fraction Division

**Definition 11.3.2** (Fraction Division Rule): Number A divided by number B (except $0$) equals A times the reciprocal of B. Given a fraction of a number, to find the number, use division.

#### Work Problems

**Definition 11.3.3** (Work Problems): Treat total work as unit "$1$"; work rate is the reciprocal of work time. Combined time $=$ total work $\div$ sum of rates.

### Unit 4: Negative Numbers in Life

#### Understanding Negative Numbers

**Definition 11.4.1** (Positive and Negative Numbers): Numbers like $6$,$4.7$,$\frac{3}{5}$ are positive; numbers like $-6$,$-4.7$,$-\frac{3}{5}$ with a leading "$-$" are negative. $0$ is neither positive nor negative.

**Definition 11.4.2** (Positive/Negative for Opposite Quantities): Define one quantity as positive; its opposite as negative. E.g. income positive, expense negative; above zero positive, below zero negative; above sea level positive, below negative.

### Unit 5: Circles

#### Understanding Circles

**Definition 11.5.1** (Parts of a Circle): The compass point fixed point is the center, $O$; the segment from center to any point on the circle is the radius, $r$; the segment through the center with both ends on the circle is the diameter, $d$. In the same circle, $d=2r$. Mozi said "a circle has one center and equal radii."

**Definition 11.5.2** (Arc, Sector, Central Angle): The part between two points on a circle is an arc; the figure bounded by an arc and two radii through its endpoints is a sector; the angle at the center is the central angle. In the same circle, sector size relates to central angle size.

#### Circumference

**Definition 11.5.3** (Pi): The circumference of any circle divided by its diameter is a fixed number called pi, $\pi$. It is an infinite nonrepeating decimal; use $\pi\approx3.14$.

**Definition 11.5.4** (Circle Circumference): The length of the curve enclosing a circle is its circumference, $C=\pi d=2\pi r$.

#### Circle Area

**Definition 11.5.5** (Circle Area): The area the circle occupies. Divide the circle into equal sectors and rearrange into an approximate rectangle, length $\pi r$, width $r$, so $S=\pi r^2$. Annulus area $=\pi(R^2-r^2)$.

### Unit 6: Mathematics in Sports

#### $400$ m Track

**Definition 11.6.1** (Track Circumference Difference): A standard $400\, \text{m}$ track has the same straight sections in each lane; the circumference difference is in the semicircular ends. Adjacent lanes are $1.25\, \text{m}$ wide, so the outer lane's curve radius is $1.25\, \text{m}$ larger; adjacent lanes differ by $1.25\times2\pi\approx7.85\, \text{m}$ per lap.

### Unit 7: Percentages

#### Meaning of Percentages

**Definition 11.7.1** (Percentages): Numbers like $14\%$,$65.5\%$,$120\%$ are percentages, meaning one number is how many hundredths of another. Percentages are also called percent rates or percentage; they cannot carry units.

**Definition 11.7.2** (Percent Rates): Pass rate $=$ pass count $\div$ total; germination rate $=$ germinated seeds $\div$ total test seeds; attendance rate $=$ attendees $\div$ expected attendees; hit rate $=$ hits $\div$ total shots.

**Definition 11.7.3** (Discounts and Cheng Numbers): A discount of several tenths means several tens percent. "Seven zhe" means selling at $70\%$ of original price; "two point five cheng" means $25\%$.

**Definition 11.7.4** (Tax Rates and Interest Rates): Tax rate is the ratio of tax to taxable income; interest rate is the ratio of interest to principal over unit time. Interest $=$ principal $\times$ rate $\times$ time.

### Unit 8: Water Is the Source of Life

#### Dripping Experiment and Water Use Estimation

**Definition 11.8.1** (Water Use Estimation): First measure from a small sample (one minute, one household), then scale by time or people to an hour, day, year, whole class, or whole school. $1\, \mathrm{L}=1000\, \mathrm{mL}$, $1\, \mathrm{m}^3=1000\, \mathrm{L}$.

### Unit 9: Review and Connection

#### Math Corner: Pigeonhole Problems

**Definition 11.9.1** (Pigeonhole / Drawer Principle): Putting $m$ objects into $n$ drawers ($m>n$), some drawer has at least $\lceil\frac{m}{n}\rceil$ objects. Calculation: $m\div n=q\cdots\cdots r$; if $r>0$, at least $q+1$; if $r=0$, at least $q$.

## Sixth Grade, Volume 2

### Unit 1: Negative Numbers

#### Understanding Negative Numbers

**Definition 12.1.1** (Positive and Negative Numbers): To represent opposite quantities (e.g. above/below zero temperature, income/expense), two kinds of numbers are needed. Previously learned numbers (e.g. $6$, $500$, $4.7$, $\frac{3}{8}$) are positive; numbers with "$-$" added (e.g. $-6$, $-500$, $-4.7$, $-\frac{3}{8}$) are negative. The leading "$+$" on positive numbers may be omitted. $0$ is neither positive nor negative.

**Definition 12.1.2** (Reading Positive/Negative Numbers): For negatives, read "negative" first, then the number; e.g. $-3$ is "negative three," $-\frac{3}{8}$ is "negative three eighths." With "$+$" it is read "positive," e.g. $+3$ is "positive three"; usually "$+$" is omitted.

**Definition 12.1.3** (Number Line for Positive/Negative Numbers): Use points on an arrowed line to represent numbers; $0$ is the origin, numbers right of $0$ are positive, numbers left of $0$ are negative. Positive and negative represent opposite quantities; opposite directions mean opposite signs.

### Unit 2: Percentages (Part 2)

#### Discounts and Cheng Numbers

**Definition 12.2.1** (Discounts): Shops reducing prices is "discounting." Several zhe means several tenths, i.e. several tens percent. Nine zhe means $90\%$ of the original price; eight-five zhe means $85\%$. Current price $=$ original price $\times$ discount.

**Definition 12.2.2** (Cheng Numbers): Cheng means one number is several tenths of another, called "several cheng." One cheng is one tenth, i.e. $10\%$; two cheng is $20\%$; three point five cheng is $35\%$.

#### Tax Rates and Interest Rates

**Definition 12.2.3** (Tax Rates): Tax paid is the tax amount; the ratio of tax to taxable income is the tax rate. Tax $=$ taxable income $\times$ rate.

**Definition 12.2.4** (Interest Rate and Interest): Money deposited in a bank is principal; extra money paid on withdrawal is interest; the ratio of interest to principal over unit time is the interest rate. Interest $=$ principal $\times$ rate $\times$ time; money returned at maturity $=$ principal $+$ interest.

### Unit 3: Life and Percentages

#### Permille and Permyriad

**Definition 12.3.1** (Permille and Permyriad): Permille means one number is per-thousandth of another, written "‰"; permyriad means per-ten-thousandth, written "‱." Like percentages, just change the denominator to $1000$ or $10000$.

### Unit 4: Cylinders and Cones

#### Recognizing Cylinders

**Definition 12.4.1** (Recognizing Cylinders): A cylinder is bounded by $3$ faces: top and bottom are identical circles; the curved side is a curved surface; the distance between the two bases is the height.

#### Cylinder Surface Area

**Definition 12.4.2** (Cylinder Lateral and Surface Area): Unrolling the cylinder's side along the height gives a rectangle whose length equals the base circumference and width equals the height. Lateral area $=$ base circumference $\times$ height $=2\pi rh$; one base area $=\pi r^2$; surface area $=$ lateral area $+$ two base areas.

#### Cylinder Volume

**Definition 12.4.3** (Cylinder Volume): Divide the cylinder base into equal sectors, cut, and rearrange into an approximate cuboid; base area equals cylinder base area, height equals cylinder height. Cylinder volume $=$ base area $\times$ height $=Sh=\pi r^2h$.

#### Cone Recognition and Volume

**Definition 12.4.4** (Recognizing Cones): A cone has a circular base and a curved side; the distance from the vertex to the base center is the height.

**Definition 12.4.5** (Cone Volume): Experimentally, a cone with equal base and height fills a cylinder exactly $3$ times; cone volume is $\frac{1}{3}$ of the equal-base equal-height cylinder. Cone volume $=\frac{1}{3}Sh=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$.

### Unit 5: Ratios

#### Meaning and Basic Property of Ratios

**Definition 12.5.1** (Meaning of Ratios): An expression equating two ratios is a proportion. The four numbers are terms; the outer two are extremes, the inner two are means. E.g. $2.4:1.6=60:40$, with $2.4$ and $40$ as extremes, $1.6$ and $60$ as means.

**Definition 12.5.2** (Basic Property and Solving Proportions): In a proportion, the product of extremes equals the product of means. If $a:b=c:d$, then $ad=bc$. Given three terms, finding the unknown is solving the proportion.

#### Direct and Inverse Proportion

**Definition 12.5.3** (Direct Proportion): Two related quantities changing together, if their ratio is constant, are in direct proportion; relation is direct proportion. In letters, $\frac{y}{x}=k$ (constant). The graph is a line through the origin.

**Definition 12.5.4** (Inverse Proportion): Two related quantities, if their product is constant, are in inverse proportion; relation is inverse proportion. In letters, $xy=k$ (constant). The graph is a smooth curve.

#### Scale

**Definition 12.5.5** (Scale): The ratio of map distance to actual distance is the scale. Map distance $:$ actual distance $=$ scale. For convenience, usually write scale with first or last term as $1$.

### Unit 6: Mathematics in Bicycles

#### Bicycle Gear Transmission

**Definition 12.6.1** (Mathematics in Bicycles): Pedals drive the front chainring, which drives the rear sprocket, which drives the rear wheel. Front teeth $\times$ front turns $=$ rear teeth $\times$ rear turns, i.e. rear turns $=\dfrac{\text{前齿轮齿数}}{\text{后齿轮齿数}}\times$ front turns. Distance per pedal revolution $=\dfrac{\text{前齿轮齿数}}{\text{后齿轮齿数}}\times$ wheel circumference. Same pedal revolutions, larger front/rear teeth ratio means farther.

### Unit 7: Math Corner — Pigeonhole Problems

#### Pigeonhole Principle

**Definition 12.7.1** (Pigeonhole Principle): Putting more than $n$ objects into $n$ drawers, some drawer has at least $2$ objects. Putting more than $kn$ objects into $n$ drawers, some drawer has at least $k+1$ objects. Calculation: divide objects by drawers; if quotient $k$ remainder $r$($r>0$), some drawer has at least $k+1$.

### Unit 8: Organization and Review

#### Numbers and Algebra

**Definition 12.8.1** (Number Classification and Meaning): Integers include positive integers,$0$, negative integers; natural numbers are positive integers and $0$. Fractions include terminating and repeating decimals. $0$ is neither positive nor negative. Dividing unit $1$ equally into several parts, one or several parts is a fraction; a number that is some percent of another is a percentage.

**Definition 12.8.2** (Operation Laws): Commutative addition $a+b=b+a$; associative addition $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$; commutative multiplication $ab=ba$; associative multiplication $(ab)c=a(bc)$; distributive $(a+b)c=ac+bc$. Use laws to simplify.

**Definition 12.8.3** (Equations): An equation with an unknown is an equation. Adding or subtracting the same number from both sides keeps equality; multiplying both sides by the same number, or dividing by the same number that is not $0$, keeps equality.

#### Shapes and Geometry

**Definition 12.8.4** (Common Shape Perimeter/Area/Volume Formulas): Rectangle $C=2(a+b)$, $S=ab$; square $C=4a$, $S=a^2$; parallelogram $S=ah$; triangle $S=\frac{1}{2}ah$; trapezoid $S=\frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$; circle $C=2\pi r$, $S=\pi r^2$; cuboid $V=abh$; cube $V=a^3$; cylinder $V=Sh$; cone $V=\frac{1}{3}Sh$.
